# Week 6 Log — Audience Segmentation and China-Market Hypotheses

**Prepared:** 16 November 2026  
**Status:** Retrospective working log; no channel analytics or Week 6 interviews were supplied.

The least obvious insight in this week’s work is that localization is not mainly a language task. It changes who the content treats as the decision-maker and what kind of reassurance feels respectful. For Fari, a direct appeal to filial responsibility might sound powerful on paper, but it risks turning family care into guilt and speaking over the older adult. A more careful starting point is a question the family can discuss together: what interaction feels comfortable, what should remain private, and who decides when the robot is used? That frame is a hypothesis to test, not a claim about Chinese or Chinese-American families as a whole.

The evidence available for this package is limited. The internship plan asks for Rogers’ adopter categories and identifies audience anchors such as older adults and caregivers for Fari and educators, parents, and administrators for Senpai. It does not provide validated segment sizes, interviews, or historic channel results. I therefore treated innovators, early adopters, early majority, and late majority as observable adoption behaviors rather than demographic labels. I also left dashboard actuals blank. The workbook proposes a four-week baseline and relative pilot thresholds so future decisions can use real account data instead of invented benchmarks.

The channel plan creates a testable distinction: Red Book content can invite a practical family conversation through a saveable checklist or scenario, while WeChat can support a detailed Senpai guide in an Official Account article and a short teacher-led scenario on Channels. These formats are recommendations, not proven platform norms. Account access and analytics must be checked before the pilot, and any public story must be real and approved rather than a fictional testimonial.

My next step would be to validate the language with Chinese-speaking caregivers and older adults directly, including people with different family roles and language preferences. I would ask what feels useful, intrusive, or guilt-inducing, then revise the examples before publishing. For Senpai, I would test whether teachers and parents need clearer information about adult oversight, privacy, and development stage before they care about a future-facing AI message. The goal is not to declare a cultural insight early; it is to make the next research question sharper and safer.
